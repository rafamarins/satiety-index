How the
study worked

In 1995, S.H.A. Holt and colleagues at the University of Sydney compared how filling 38 foods were.

Each test portion contained 1,000 kJ, about 240 calories. Groups of 11 to 13 people rated how full they felt every 15 minutes for two hours. Researchers combined those ratings into a score, with white bread set at 100%.

    Beyond the original index

    What newer
    research says

    Reviewed 26 October 2026

    Fullness also depends on food texture, preparation and the rest of the meal.

    The 38 scores above remain the 1995 results. These newer studies use different methods and cannot be combined into an updated ranking.

    October 2026 issue

    43 men · Four test meals

    Texture changes the fullness response

    A harder-textured ultra-processed meal produced a 32% greater fullness response over two hours than its softer counterpart, at the same calories. Participants also ate less at the snack afterwards.

    Keep in mind: Total daily calorie intake did not differ significantly. Results apply to these test meals.

    July 2026

    15 adults · New Zealand pilot

    The rest of the meal matters

    Adding lamb or soy protein to potato, pasta or rice suppressed appetite more than eating the carbohydrate foods alone. Both protein sources had similar effects on appetite.

    Keep in mind: This small trial measured short-term appetite, not subsequent food intake or weight change.

    Read the protein and mixed-meal study ↗

    Online November 2025

    41 adults · Two 14-day diets

    Slower-eating foods can reduce intake

    Participants ate around 369 fewer calories per day on an ultra-processed menu with textures that slowed eating, compared with a faster-eating menu. The difference persisted across the two weeks.

    Keep in mind: Researchers changed food textures, not instructions to chew slowly. Long-term weight loss was not tested.

    Read the eating-rate trial ↗

    2024

    26 adults · Potato versus rice meals

    Potatoes in mixed meals

    With meatballs or a vegetarian substitute, participants ate 23–25% fewer calories at meals with instant mash or baked fries than with rice. Including a pizza meal two hours later, total intake was still 13–16% lower.

    Keep in mind: A short-term, potato-industry-funded trial of specific preparations, not evidence of long-term weight loss.

    Read the potato and rice study ↗

    Feeling fuller, eating less and losing weight over time are different outcomes. These trials do not measure all three.
